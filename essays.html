Can Poetry Matter? (Is Coding Dead?)

Author's note: Originally written as a critical essay on twentieth-century poetics, this argument reads today like a field report on the "coding is dead" debate. Whenever people ask whether software engineers should still hand-write code in an era of automated generation, we are re-litigating Edmund Wilson's question about verse and prose. A deliberate, hand-crafted medium does not die when a broader workhorse absorbs its routine duties; it concentrates into craft communities where "thought tends to collect in pools." (See the 2026 postscript below.)

During the twentieth century, a rather disturbing discussion began to develop about the state of poetry in modern literature. This discussion centered on a question whose answer would have significant implications in mainstream culture, and which can be simplified down to: "Can Poetry Matter?" It is a question that centers on the idea that poetry may no longer be relevant to the public, with scholars attempting to figure out why this has occurred and whether it can be reversed. Poetry was once a major pillar of literature, so its fade from the public eye is quite alarming and has many enthusiasts nervous. In this paper, however, I aim to argue that poetry is still very much relevant post-modernism. I will do this by examining Edmund Wilson's "Is Verse a Dying Technique?", Joseph Epstein's follow-up essay "Who Killed Poetry?", and the more recent update in Dana Gioia's "Can Poetry Matter?" All three were controversial essays that garnered wide responses and broke the discussion open. Yet all three seemingly arrived at the unfortunate conclusion that poetry is no longer relevant, a claim I aim to refute.

A good place to begin is Edmund Wilson's essay, "Is Verse a Dying Technique?", the first major treatment of this topic to draw broad attention. Early on, Wilson writes: "The important thing to recognize, it seems to me, is that the literary technique of verse was once made to serve many purposes for which we now, as a rule, use prose." This assertion, and the arguments that follow it, advance the idea that prose is displacing verse, essentially pushing verse out of relevance because writers are choosing prose in domains where they would once have chosen verse. Wilson illustrates that centuries ago verse was used in fields like history and science that would look downright out of place in verse today. He locates the shift at a clear turning point: "when we arrive at the nineteenth century, a new concept of poetry appears... the romantics were to redefine poetry." From here, Wilson argues that poetry became highly specialized and "immediate." Long works like Homer's epics or Shakespeare's plays, written in verse, no longer fit easily inside that narrower definition. Writers of high caliber began turning to prose for major works where, a century earlier, they might have chosen verse. Observing this migration, Wilson poses the questions at the heart of the debate:

"What then is the cause of this disuse into which the verse technique has been falling for at least the last two hundred years? And what are we to expect in the future? Is verse to be limited now to increasingly specialized functions and go out all together? Or will it recover the domains it has lost?"
— Edmund Wilson

Joseph Epstein follows Wilson's inquiry with his own essay, "Who Killed Poetry?" The title alone signals a sharper posture: where Wilson asked whether verse was dying, Epstein seems to accept that it is already dead. Epstein opens by recalling the near-religious authority poetry once commanded, only to argue that this power vanished after the 1950s. Poets just prior to that period were "the first living poets to be given the full academic treatment," becoming celebrated figures inside universities where their work was taught. Those poets remained grounded in mainstream literary culture. Yet Epstein contends that the poets who immediately followed them "were less sure; they knew something had gone wrong. And they were right. It had." He grants that more poets teach at universities, poetry readings are more common, and more poetry is published than ever before, only to counter with a devastating caveat: "But what of the vacuum? I should say that it consists generally of this: that however much contemporary poetry may be honored, it is, outside a very small circle, scarcely read." Surveying explanations ranging from poetry's obscurity to a distracted national culture, Epstein turns directly on the poets themselves, noting that the usual excuses "seem to let the poets themselves off the hook." Volume alone does not equal critical quality. Echoing Wilson on prose's ascent, Epstein concludes that poetry has been "threatened by having been taken out of the world, chilled in the classroom, and vastly overproduced by men and women who are licensed to write it by degree if not necessarily by talent or spirit." On his account, poets are at fault, and poetry will not recover until better poems are written.

The most prominent modern entry in this lineage is Dana Gioia's controversial essay, "Can Poetry Matter?" Gioia largely accepts Wilson's and Epstein's premise of public retreat from his opening lines: "American poetry now belongs to a subculture. No longer part of the mainstream of artistic and intellectual life, it has become the specialized occupation of a relatively small and isolated group." Where Gioia differs is in asking earnestly whether poetry can regain a public audience. Like Epstein, he observes record publication alongside vanishing mainstream presence: major newspapers and journals overwhelmingly review novels while brushing past books of verse. And like Epstein, Gioia faults poets and critics for a flood of mediocrity, warning that "one can easily miss a radiant poem amid the many lackluster ones." Reviewers have grown soft, flattering peers rather than evaluating work rigorously. As Gioia admonishes, "by abandoning the hard work of evaluation, the poetry subculture demeans its own art," adding by contrast that "the reviewers of fifty years ago knew that their primary loyalty must lie not with their fellow poets or publishers but with the reader." Poetry has drifted from the public view as prose expanded, yet is the poet truly to blame?

While I agree with Wilson, Epstein, and Gioia that poetry occupies a smaller footprint in mainstream culture than it once did, I do not agree with their diagnosis of why this happened. Against Wilson, I do not believe prose became verse's adversary trying to drive it from relevance. Rather, poetry transformed until verse was no longer the right vehicle for sprawling narrative or expository works. Wilson notes that Romanticism marked the start of this shift, and Gioia states the mechanism even more clearly: "Romanticism's emphasis on intensity made poetry seem so 'fleeting and quintessential' that eventually it dwindled into a mainly lyric medium." This was not prose usurping verse; it was verse refining its own purpose and clearing room for prose to carry long-form weight. That distinction matters. If writers chose prose for histories, essays, and novels, they likely made the right choice for the medium. Verse gained greater concentration as a lyric art, while prose, less bound by strict formal constraint, gave writers room to build expansive works of literature. What we witnessed was an evolution of literary ecology, two mediums finding their rightful domains.

As for Epstein's and Gioia's claim that poets and critics betrayed the art by ceasing to produce "good poetry," that explanation also falls short. The dynamic between poets and critics today is a natural byproduct of Modernism's legacy. A central pillar of Modernist poetry was lyric self-expression, placing the individual poet's interior life, perception, and voice at the center of the poem. Once personal expression across wide stylistic differences became the sovereign aim, fragmentation into smaller, distinct communities was inevitable. Far from being a catastrophe, that plurality allows individual readers to discover voices and traditions that speak directly to them.

Jan Schreiber dismantles the tidy notion of objectively sorting "good" from "bad" poetry in his essay "Poetry and the Problem of Standards." Schreiber asks: "In principle, is it possible, faced with an overwhelming body of work in print, to cull out excellent poems in the way one can cull out fine diamonds or superb soufflés?" For diamonds or soufflés, specialists apply agreed-upon, largely objective criteria. With literature, standards resist mechanical sorting. Exceptionally bad work is obvious enough, yet across the decent, the great, and the enduring, disagreement is inherent, especially when poetry breaks unfamiliar ground. As Schreiber writes, "given that we can know only retrospectively that a particular poem was a decisive experience for us, how can we hope to determine, even in theory, what we ought to be looking for?" He likens choosing poems to choosing friends: we are drawn to "general characteristics we tend to admire on the basis of disposition and past experience." In an art as subjective as post-Modernist poetry, listeners from different lives will naturally diverge on what moves them. If a poem genuinely transforms a reader, it has done its work. And people of shared sensibility gather around those works together. In his closing paragraph, Schreiber captures this exact phenomenon: "minds inevitably seek out and feed off other minds. Because they do so with discrimination—drawing close to some and avoiding others—communities emerge, schools form, and thought tends to collect in pools."

Verse is not dead. Nobody killed poetry. And poetry matters. What we are witnessing is simply poetry moving through another phase of its life. With more people than ever writing poetry, one can only conclude that more people than ever are reading it, only across far more varied pools rather than under a single canonical figure like Yeats or T. S. Eliot. Perhaps it is as Donald Hall wrote in "Death to the Death of Poetry," answering Wilson's and Epstein's premature eulogies: "our trouble is not with poetry but with the public perception of poetry." Poetry does not need to force its way back to the center of mass entertainment; it is entirely vital as a subculture. Poetry remains essential and alive, just not in the uniform way the mainstream public expects to see it.

Postscript (2026) · Field Note
On hand-written craft & the "death of coding"

"Writing is not mere transcription of a finished thought; it is the instrument of thought itself."

Re-reading this essay alongside the current refrain that "coding is dead" and the earnest question of whether anyone should still hand-write code, the parallels are uncanny. Just as Edmund Wilson watched prose take over history, exposition, and narrative from verse, natural-language prompting and model-generated syntax are absorbing routine CRUD scaffolding, glue code, and throwaway scripts. That migration does not kill hand-written code any more than prose killed poetry; it clarifies what hand-written code is actually for.

Joseph Epstein and Dana Gioia warned that overproduction paired with soft criticism buries radiant work under mediocrity. Today's repositories face that exact flood: generated output is cheap, plentiful, and plausible at a glance, while rigorous code review (whose primary loyalty lies with the system's reader and the customer) is rarer than ever. When anyone can summon five hundred lines in seconds, taste, boundary discipline, and knowing which lines should never exist become the real scarcity.

You still hand-write code for the same reason a poet still works line by line in meter or free verse. Typing out algebraic types in OCaml, tracing pointer lifetimes in C, or shaping a Saga compensation path forces your mind against edge cases that high-level prose smooths over. Coding is not dead. Like verse after Modernism, hand-written craft is simply shedding its mass-production chores and gathering in smaller, more demanding pools.

I · Infrastructure economics & capital allocation 2025-11 · ~4 min read

The frugal architect's ledger

I. The Context: Modern cloud software often defaults to unchecked distributed decomposition. Engineers instantiate managed data warehouses, partitioned message queues, and Kubernetes control planes before their product has served its first hundred concurrent requests. What begins as an ambition for infinite elasticity inevitably transforms into an unsustainable cloud invoice that impairs startup gross margins.

II. The Core Thesis: Cost is not an accounting metric reviewed at the end of the quarter; it is a design constraint. If I cannot roughly price a single API request, I do not understand the system yet — mechanically or commercially.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): Rather than maintaining idle Snowflake or Redshift clusters alongside third-party APM tools, Ariadne marries live observability with zero-copy historical analytics. An in-memory telemetry ring delivers real-time operational diagnostics and request-level metrics locally, while structured transactional event logs pipe directly to compressed Parquet partitions on S3. When CRM analytics or audit trails are queried, an embedded DuckDB worker executes in-process SQL over the object store. Tail-latency drops to sub-second localized execution while idle cloud spend collapses to zero.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Nothing comes without sacrifice. In-process DuckDB instances and telemetry ring buffers share the memory footprint of the host process. A poorly structured analytical aggregation can induce memory pressure on adjacent transaction handlers if concurrency limits are omitted. We accept the friction of manual memory bounding and S3 cold-fetch latency in exchange for deleting entire multi-thousand-dollar managed clusters.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: This ledger holds true until analytical query volume approaches continuous saturation across gigabyte-scale datasets. When concurrent query load threatens transactional latency envelopes, the analytical workload will be extracted to an isolated worker pool, and not before.

II · Functional domain design 2025-09 · ~4 min read

Functional domain modeling & type-safe workflows

I. The Context: Enterprise backends are frequently riddled with invisible execution paths: unhandled exceptions, null pointer surprises, and nested try/catch wrappers that mask logic errors under the guise of resilience. When an exception is thrown five call frames deep, tracing the causal flow is an exercise in archaeological excavation.

II. The Core Thesis: A business use case is a series of transformations on two tracks — success and failure. Functions take an input and return Result('data, 'domain_error). A failure should leave the happy path without crashing the thread or skipping the compensation that the domain actually requires.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): In our OCaml checkout engine, the entire order intake is expressed as a monadic composition: validate_cart >=> reserve_inventory >=> authorize_payment >=> persist_order. If authorize_payment yields CardDeclined, the execution never touches persist_order; it passes directly to the error branch handler, triggering an immediate inventory release. The compiler refuses to compile the pipeline if any potential domain error is omitted.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Writing idiomatic functional pipelines requires an upfront cognitive reorientation for engineers accustomed to procedural exceptions. Composing asynchronous I/O with monadic result types (Result.t Lwt.t) requires strict combinator discipline. You exchange runtime chaos for compiler-time ceremony.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: For trivial utility scripts or throwaway scrapers, establishing formal ADTs and railway tracks represents needless overhead. However, wherever financial transactions, inventory reserves, or legal compliance are at stake, functional DDD is non-negotiable.

III · Distributed consistency 2025-08 · ~4 min read

Sagas over two-phase commit

I. The Context: Multi-step transaction processing across decoupled services frequently tempts engineers into reaching for distributed Two-Phase Commit (2PC) or centralized coordination orchestrators like Temporal or AWS Step Functions. However, distributed transactions across networks with intermittent latency violate the CAP theorem's availability guarantees.

II. The Core Thesis: Network boundaries are hostile and asynchronous. Consistency usually cannot be forced through global locks; it has to be earned with explicit compensating actions and local state transitions.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): We implemented a native Saga orchestration engine in OCaml. Instead of deploying an external workflow daemon, each transaction stage is encoded as a discrete event on an in-memory queue backed by PostgreSQL transactional outboxes. If payment processing succeeds but inventory allocation subsequently fails, the system executes explicit domain compensations (reversing charges, releasing holds) without global locking.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: The operational cost is shifted squarely into engineering diligence. A developer cannot rely on database-level ROLLBACK. Every domain action requires writing, testing, and verifying an idempotent compensating counterpart. If an unhandled network partition stalls an edge compensation, manual reconciliation routines must be available.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: The native in-memory saga pattern functions flawlessly within a modular monolith handling hundreds of concurrent multi-step orders. Should asynchronous transactions swell into thousands of heterogeneous microservices across separate corporate boundaries, dedicated orchestration machinery will be reconsidered.

See also the canon shelf on distributed systems.

IV · Layered modularity & fault isolation 2025-04 · ~4 min read

Clean layering & fault isolation in backend systems

I. The Context: Web applications routinely succumb to the "big ball of mud" syndrome. Database drivers, third-party payment SDKs, and transport protocols become tightly entwined with core business rules. When a vendor API alters its payload schema or throws an undocumented timeout, entire backend workers crash.

II. The Core Thesis: Systems break when flaky peripherals share a blast radius with core domain operations. Keep the kernel boring. Put network protocols, drivers, and vendor APIs behind adapters so a bad payload dies at the rim.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): Our custom Ariadne framework structures the backend as a modular monolith with OCaml functors. The domain engine is parameterized over abstract signatures; Stripe, Shippo, and mailers live as outer implementations. If a driver throws on a bad payload, the blast radius should stop at the adapter.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Maintaining pure abstraction barriers requires mapping domain entities to adapter DTOs back and forth. You write additional translation boilerplate rather than passing database records directly to the HTTP serializer. We gladly pay this structural translation cost to guarantee total fault isolation.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: For simple CRUD applications with minimal lifespan, maintaining strict port/adapter isolation may introduce premature abstraction drag. But for core domain systems expected to survive years of third-party vendor churn, Tanenbaum's layered isolation philosophy is essential.

V · Management & apprenticeship 2025-01 · ~4 min read

The economics of simplicity & engineering apprenticeship

I. The Context: Engineering organizations often believe that hiring more developers directly accelerates velocity. In practice, Brooks' Law reliably takes hold: adding personnel to a complex, undisciplined architecture creates quadratic communication drag and exponentially higher defect injection rates.

II. The Core Thesis: Teams do not scale through headcount sprawl; they scale through a system small enough to teach. When the architecture is simple and the types are strict, a small aligned group can outbuild a much larger one that spends its week in coordination.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): Rather than treating architecture as ivory-tower dogma, we run an apprenticeship model. Senior engineers pair directly with teammates across production RFCs, domain refactors, and compiler errors. Because the modular monolith has zero distributed daemon sprawl, a new engineer can run the entire business suite locally on their laptop in under two minutes and push verified code on week one.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Apprenticeship requires active senior time. It means choosing live pairing over passive pull-request comments and taking time to teach the commercial rationale behind architectural boundaries. The trade-off is an upfront leadership time investment in exchange for compounding team autonomy and near-zero turnover.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: In massive organizations with thousands of loosely coordinated contractors, standardized bureaucratic processes and rigid microservice silos may be forced upon teams to contain organizational boundaries. For high-leverage product teams, deliberate simplicity and close apprenticeship remain unbeatable.
