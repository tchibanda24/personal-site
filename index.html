On unikernels, MirageOS & the limits of optimization

"We can only see a short distance ahead, but we can see plenty there that needs to be done."

— Alan Turing

In exploring MirageOS—a library operating system that compiles OCaml code directly into bootable, single-purpose unikernels—the architectural appeal is undeniable. You eliminate hundreds of megabytes of unused POSIX user-space, strip away shell attack vectors, and obtain microsecond boot times with a negligible RAM footprint.

However, sound engineering requires resisting the temptation of pure technical fetishism. It is easy to fall into the trap of "optimization monkey coding"—spending weeks shaving kilobytes off a binary or micro-optimizing execution paths when the primary bottleneck is human comprehension or business context alignment.

Unikernels are a masterclass in radical minimalism. But in production, radical ideas must earn their keep. We choose OCaml and modular monoliths not because they are academically pure, but because they enforce strict type safety and domain boundaries while keeping total operational cognitive load low for a lean team.

Systems in production

pahukama.com

Modular monolith & distributed saga engine

OCaml · domain-driven design

Context. Built for pahukama.com to handle distributed e-commerce transactions without relying on heavy third-party workflow orchestrators.

Design. Asynchronous messaging bus with explicit compensation logic, short-lived JWT tokens, and database TTL bounds to enforce idempotency across intermittent networks.

Outcome. Zero orchestrator daemon overhead; processed 500+ transactions with fault tolerance built directly into OCaml's static type system.

Trade-off. Every compensation is a code path we must design and test ourselves; the framework gives nothing for free. In exchange, there is no extra infrastructure to operate at 3 a.m.

Embedded analytics & zero-copy lakehouse

DuckDB · AWS S3

Context. E-commerce analytics often suffer from expensive warehouse costs (Snowflake/Redshift) during idle periods.

Design. Streamed event logs directly as Parquet files to AWS S3, executing zero-copy in-memory SQL queries via embedded DuckDB instance within the backend runtime.

Outcome. Sub-second CRM analytical queries at near-zero recurring infrastructure cost.

Trade-off. Analytical queries share the backend's memory envelope. We bound concurrency and accept S3 cold-start latency in exchange for zero idle warehouse spend.

Resilient projections & state idempotency

HTMX · server-side rendering

Context. Eliminating client-side SPA state divergence and heavy JavaScript bundles.

Design. Server-side rendered HTML fragments coupled with HTMX for declarative DOM updates, protected by token-backed request deduplication.

Outcome. One language on the server, no hydration bugs, mutations rendered idempotent at the edge.

Trade-off. Interactions pay a server round-trip; we accept ~80 ms p50 latency in exchange for deleting an entire client-side state layer.

The architect of simplicity

operating principles

Anyone can make a software system complicated. Adding another microservice, message broker, or generic abstraction layer is the path of least resistance. Simplicity is the hardest discipline in software engineering. It requires ruthlessly interrogating assumptions, understanding commercial unit economics, refusing speculative complexity, and teaching teams to master system fundamentals.

These principles form the operating framework for how we engineer platforms, allocate capital, and mentor teams at pahukama.com.

I · Methodological reduction — René Descartes (1637)

Strip inherited dogma to undeniable first principles

Never adopt an architectural pattern because an industry consensus decreed it. Reject the default sprawl of microservices, distributed coordinators, and managed clusters until your domain proves vertical exhaustion. Divide each problem into as many parts as necessary to resolve it cleanly, and verify every interface boundary with unyielding skepticism.

II · Mathematical boundary defense — Michael Sipser / Alan Turing

Make illegal states unrepresentable at compile time

State transitions must mirror formal state machines. Algebraic Data Types (ADTs) and expressive type systems should police domain invariants so that invalid business states fail at compilation rather than generating cryptic 3 a.m. runtime panics. Formal correctness is not academic pedantry; it is the ultimate lever of engineering velocity.

III · Frugal engineering & unit economics — Werner Vogels / Warren Buffett

Cost & tail-latency are core non-functional requirements

Every architectural decision carries a financial invoice and a tail-latency footprint. Optimize in-process memory sharing, columnar Parquet layouts, and embedded storage engines before paying the multi-thousand-dollar cloud tax for managed distributed analytics. A frugal architecture protects gross margins and survives economic shifts; an undisciplined one drowns in infrastructure rent.

IV · Inversion & mental models — Charlie Munger

Invert, always invert: design against ruin first

As Charlie Munger observed, many complex problems are solved only when addressed backwards. Instead of asking how to make a distributed system succeed, first ask what will inevitably destroy it: split-brain states, unbound memory spikes, data corruption, and operational burnout. Design invariants that structurally preclude catastrophic outcomes before optimizing the happy path.

V · Deliberate velocity ("slow is smooth, smooth is fast") — Cal Newport

Haste is the enemy of real engineering velocity

Frantic, hype-driven development produces fragile, debt-ridden software that grinds progress to a halt within months. We subscribe to Cal Newport's ethos of slow productivity: do fewer things, focus deeply on domain invariants, and prioritize structural soundness. When you move deliberately through strict type boundaries, you do not spend half your week troubleshooting production emergencies.

VI · Decentralized simplicity — Pieter Hintjens (ZeroMQ)

Prefer dumb pipes and smart endpoints over centralized daemons

Centralized brokers and global coordinators create brittle operational coupling. As Pieter Hintjens proved, systems scale cleanly when messaging protocols are localized, asynchronous, and point-to-point. Compensations, retries, and idempotency belong within the domain actors themselves—never delegated to an invisible third-party workflow daemon.

VII · Functional domain-driven design — Scott Wlaschin

Types as executable ubiquitous language

Domain models should not be buried under heavyweight object hierarchies or ORM abstractions. As Scott Wlaschin demonstrated, the domain model is an executable ubiquitous language composed of pure types: records for logical compositions, variants for distinct business choices, and total functions for workflows. Code documents business reality faithfully because the type system leaves no room for ambiguity.

VIII · Clean layering & fault containment — Andrew S. Tanenbaum

Isolate volatile adapters from the core domain kernel

Operating system stability relies on strict boundary layering: untrusted, flaky device drivers must not run where critical kernel abstractions live. In software systems, we apply the exact same discipline. Network protocols, database drivers, and third-party vendor APIs must remain isolated at the application rim. A panic in an external adapter should never poison or crash the core domain engine.

IX · Collaborative apprenticeship — Leadership & mentorship

Demystify abstractions through hands-on craft

Great software systems are not preserved by isolated "rockstars"; they thrive when engineering is practiced as an open apprenticeship. Senior architects must pair collaboratively, demystify compiler errors, explain the commercial reasons behind trade-offs, and give emerging engineers hands-on ownership of complex subsystems. High agency grows through shared technical mastery.

X · Evolutionary discipline — Martin Fowler (Refactoring)

Continuous restructuring trumps big-bang architectural rewrites

The best architectures are not carved into stone tablets; they evolve through disciplined, test-backed transformations. Clean code smells early, modularize within a monolith before carving physical network boundaries, and refactor relentlessly to keep cognitive drag near zero.

"Simplicity is prerequisite for reliability."

— Edsger W. Dijkstra

Extended essays

Five-point treatments bridging business economics, team leadership, and production architecture

I · Infrastructure economics & capital allocation 2025-11

The frugal architect's ledger

I. The Context: Modern cloud software often defaults to unchecked distributed decomposition. Engineers instantiate managed data warehouses, partitioned message queues, and Kubernetes control planes before their product has served its first hundred concurrent requests. What begins as an ambition for infinite elasticity inevitably transforms into an unsustainable cloud invoice that impairs startup gross margins.

II. The Core Thesis: As articulated in Werner Vogels' The Frugal Architect and Warren Buffett's capital allocation principles, cost is not an accounting metric reviewed at the end of the fiscal quarter; it is a fundamental non-functional requirement. An architect who cannot predict the dollar cost of a single API request does not understand their own system's mechanics or its commercial impact.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): Rather than maintaining idle Snowflake or Redshift clusters, we pipe structured analytical telemetry directly to compressed Parquet files stored on S3. When CRM analytics are requested, an embedded DuckDB worker queries the columnar partitions in-process. Tail-latency drops from several seconds over remote wire connections to sub-second localized execution, while idle infrastructure costs collapse to zero.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Nothing comes without sacrifice. In-process DuckDB instances share the memory footprint of the host instance. A poorly structured analytical aggregation can induce memory pressure on adjacent transaction handlers if concurrency limits are omitted. We accept the friction of manual memory bounding and S3 cold-fetch latency in exchange for deleting entire multi-thousand-dollar managed clusters.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: This ledger holds true until analytical query volume approaches continuous saturation across gigabyte-scale datasets. When concurrent query load threatens transactional latency envelopes, the analytical workload will be extracted to an isolated worker pool—not before.

Rooted in Werner Vogels & Warren Buffett.

II · Functional domain design 2025-09

Functional domain modeling & type-safe workflows

I. The Context: Enterprise backends are frequently riddled with invisible execution paths: unhandled exceptions, null pointer surprises, and nested `try/catch` wrappers that mask logic errors under the guise of resilience. When an exception is thrown five call frames deep, tracing the causal flow is an exercise in archaeological excavation.

II. The Core Thesis: Scott Wlaschin's Domain Modeling Made Functional provides the antidote: functional domain-driven design. A business use case is nothing more than a series of pure transformations connected along two tracks—Success and Failure. Functions take an input and return `Result('data, 'domain_error)`. Any failure diverts execution cleanly onto the error track without crashing the thread or skipping domain compensation hooks.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): In our OCaml checkout engine, the entire order intake is expressed as a monadic composition: validate_cart >=> reserve_inventory >=> authorize_payment >=> persist_order. If authorize_payment yields CardDeclined, the execution never touches persist_order; it passes directly to the error branch handler, triggering an immediate inventory release. The compiler refuses to compile the pipeline if any potential domain error is omitted.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Writing idiomatic functional pipelines requires an upfront cognitive reorientation for engineers accustomed to procedural exceptions. Composing asynchronous I/O with monadic result types (`Result.t Lwt.t`) requires strict combinator discipline. You exchange runtime chaos for compiler-time ceremony.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: For trivial utility scripts or throwaway scrapers, establishing formal ADTs and railway tracks represents needless overhead. However, wherever financial transactions, inventory reserves, or legal compliance are at stake, functional DDD is non-negotiable.

Rooted in Scott Wlaschin, Domain Modeling Made Functional.

III · Distributed consistency 2025-08

Sagas over two-phase commit

I. The Context: Multi-step transaction processing across decoupled services frequently tempts engineers into reaching for distributed Two-Phase Commit (2PC) or centralized coordination orchestrators like Temporal or AWS Step Functions. However, distributed transactions across networks with intermittent latency violate the CAP theorem's availability guarantees.

II. The Core Thesis: Applying Pieter Hintjens' lessons from The ZeroMQ Guide and DeCandia's Dynamo paper, we recognize that network boundaries are inherently hostile and asynchronous. Consistency cannot be forced through global locks; it must be achieved through explicit, backward-compensating actions and localized state transitions.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): We implemented a native Saga orchestration engine in OCaml. Instead of deploying an external workflow daemon, each transaction stage is encoded as a discrete event on an in-memory queue backed by PostgreSQL transactional outboxes. If payment processing succeeds but inventory allocation subsequently fails, the system executes explicit domain compensations (reversing charges, releasing holds) without global locking.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: The operational cost is shifted squarely into engineering diligence. A developer cannot rely on database-level `ROLLBACK`. Every domain action requires writing, testing, and verifying an idempotent compensating counterpart. If an unhandled network partition stalls an edge compensation, manual reconciliation routines must be available.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: The native in-memory saga pattern functions flawlessly within a modular monolith handling hundreds of concurrent multi-step orders. Should asynchronous transactions swell into thousands of heterogeneous micro-services across separate corporate boundaries, dedicated orchestration machinery will be reconsidered.

Rooted in Pieter Hintjens (ZeroMQ) & DeCandia et al. (Dynamo).

IV · Layered modularity & fault isolation 2025-04

Clean layering & fault isolation in backend systems

I. The Context: Web applications routinely succumb to the "big ball of mud" syndrome. Database drivers, third-party payment SDKs, and transport protocols become tightly entwined with core business rules. When a vendor API alters its payload schema or throws an undocumented timeout, entire backend workers crash.

II. The Core Thesis: In Modern Operating Systems, Andrew S. Tanenbaum demonstrated that system resilience depends upon strict, clean abstraction layering and modular fault isolation. Systems break when volatile peripheral dependencies run inside the same blast radius as foundational domain operations. Reliability demands keeping the core kernel pure, with peripheral I/O isolated behind rigid adapter boundaries.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): We structure our backend modular monolith following the Ports & Adapters principle. The core is our pure functional domain engine. Third-party integrations (Stripe, Shippo, SMTP mailers) run as isolated outer adapters behind strict interfaces. If an external driver encounters an unhandled payload exception, the blast radius is contained strictly at the adapter boundary without poisoning domain state.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Maintaining pure abstraction barriers requires mapping domain entities to adapter DTOs back and forth. You write additional translation boilerplate rather than passing database records directly to the HTTP serializer. We gladly pay this structural translation cost to guarantee total fault isolation.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: For simple CRUD applications with minimal lifespan, maintaining strict port/adapter isolation may introduce premature abstraction drag. But for core domain systems expected to survive years of third-party vendor churn, Tanenbaum's layered isolation philosophy is essential.

Rooted in Andrew S. Tanenbaum, Modern Operating Systems.

V · Management & apprenticeship 2025-01

The economics of simplicity & engineering apprenticeship

I. The Context: Engineering organizations often believe that hiring more developers directly accelerates velocity. In practice, Brooks' Law reliably takes hold: adding personnel to a complex, undisciplined architecture creates quadratic communication drag and exponentially higher defect injection rates.

II. The Core Thesis: Drawing from Charlie Munger's multidisciplinary mental models and Cal Newport's philosophy of deep craft, high-performing teams do not scale through head-count sprawl; they scale through radical simplicity and hands-on apprenticeship. When the system's architecture is simple and bounded by strict types, a small, tightly aligned team can easily outbuild a sprawling enterprise department.

III. Production Realities (pahukama.com): Rather than treating architecture as ivory-tower dogma, we run an apprenticeship model. Senior engineers pair directly with teammates across production RFCs, domain refactors, and compiler errors. Because the modular monolith has zero distributed daemon sprawl, a new engineer can run the entire business suite locally on their laptop in under two minutes and push verified code on week one.

IV. The Trade-Off & Friction: Apprenticeship requires active senior time. It means choosing live pairing over passive pull-request comments and taking time to teach the commercial rationale behind architectural boundaries. The trade-off is an upfront leadership time investment in exchange for compounding team autonomy and near-zero turnover.

V. Falsification & Future Bounds: In massive organizations with thousands of loosely coordinated contractors, standardized bureaucratic processes and rigid microservice silos may be forced upon teams to contain organizational boundaries. For high-leverage product teams, deliberate simplicity and close apprenticeship remain unbeatable.

Rooted in Charlie Munger & Cal Newport.
