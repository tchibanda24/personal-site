Working principles

from the notebook

These are working notes, not commandments. They are how I tend to design systems, spend money on infrastructure, and work with other engineers at pahukama.com. I change them when the work proves me wrong.

Writing them down is mostly so I cannot quietly abandon them. The bias is toward fewer moving parts, types that make illegal states hard to represent, and enough shared understanding that a small team can reason about the whole system.

I. Reduction II. Inversion III. Frugality IV. Functional DDD V. Type Bounds VI. Fault Isolation VII. Raw Hardware VIII. Evolution IX. Dumb Pipes X. Trade-offs XI. Slow Velocity XII. Apprenticeship

I · Methodological reduction

Start from the problem, not the pattern catalog

I try not to adopt a pattern because a conference talk made it sound inevitable. Microservices, coordinators, and managed clusters are tools; they are expensive ones. Split a problem far enough to see it clearly, then add distribution only when a single process is honestly exhausted.

II · Inversion

Design against ruin first

Before asking how a design succeeds, I ask what will wreck it: split-brain state, unbounded memory, silent data loss, a page nobody can debug. Put guards on those first. The happy path is easier to polish once the failure paths have names.

III · Frugality

Cost and tail latency belong in the design, not the invoice review

If I cannot roughly price a request, I do not understand the system yet. I reach for in-process memory, Parquet, and embedded engines before leasing a warehouse that idles all month. Frugality here is not aesthetic; it is how a small team keeps margin and attention.

IV · Functional DDD

Let the types speak the business language

I would rather read an order as a set of records and variants than reconstruct it from an ORM graph. Records for composition, variants for distinct choices, functions for workflows. When the types match the language the business already uses, onboarding gets shorter and arguments get clearer.

V · Type bounds

Make illegal states hard to represent

I would rather a compiler reject a bad checkout than discover it in logs. Algebraic types in OCaml and strict models in TypeScript are the same habit: put the invariant in the data, not in a comment above a switch. It is slower to write the first time and faster every week after.

VI · Fault isolation

Keep flaky I/O out of the domain kernel

Payment SDKs, mailers, and database drivers change shape without asking. I keep them behind adapters so a bad payload or timeout dies at the rim. The translation layer is real work; it is cheaper than letting a vendor timeout crash checkout.

VII · Raw hardware

Remember there is a machine under the types

Functional abstractions are only as honest as our picture of memory, caches, and syscalls. I still want to know whether a structure is a pointer chase or a tight scan. Forgetting that is how you build something elegant that falls over the first time it meets a full disk or a cold page cache.

VIII · Evolution

Modularize first; split the network later

Most of the time a clear module boundary inside one process is enough. I would rather evolve an interface in place than start with a rewrite or a service cut. Distribution is easy to add and expensive to undo.

IX · Dumb pipes

Keep retries and compensations next to the work

A central broker is sometimes right. More often it becomes the thing everyone pages at 3 a.m. I prefer local, asynchronous handoffs, with compensations and idempotency living in the domain code that knows what a failed payment actually means.

X · Trade-offs

Write down what you are sacrificing, and when you will reverse it

I do not trust "best practice" as a reason. Before adding a seam, queue, or cache, I want the characteristic we are buying, the coupling we are accepting, the thing we are giving up, and the load or team trigger that means we were wrong. Why the boundary exists matters more than how it is wired.

XI · Slow velocity

Velocity comes from doing things well the first time

Shipping a brittle path fast is not velocity if it comes back as incidents and retreads. Real speed comes from stable systems and honest abstractions you build once and stop re-litigating. Choosing a stricter stack like OCaml looks slower on day one; in production, eliminating rewrites is what lets a small team keep moving forward. Slow here means deliberate, not late on purpose.

XII · Apprenticeship

Teach the why while pairing on the work

I would rather sit with someone through a compiler error than leave a clever abstraction in a review comment. Pairing, walking the commercial reason for a boundary, and handing over a real subsystem beats a slide deck. Teams get faster when more than one person can hold the design.
